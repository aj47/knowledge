## human\n\nHow can I prepare my legs for a tennis match to be both relaxed yet not tired\n
## assistant\n\nGreat question — especially with your knee considerations. Here's a practical pre-match leg prep routine:

**Timing: 20-30 minutes before play**

**1. Light activation (5 min)**
- Brisk walk or easy jog to get blood flowing
- Skip the static stretching — it can actually reduce power output before explosive activity

**2. Dynamic mobility (5 min)**
- Leg swings (forward/back and side-to-side, 10 each leg)
- Walking lunges with a twist — gentle, not deep
- Lateral shuffles and carioca (grapevine) steps
- Ankle circles — important for court movement

**3. Neural activation without fatigue (3-5 min)**
- 3-4 short sprints at ~70% effort (10-15 meters) — this "wakes up" fast-twitch fibers without draining glycogen
- A few split-step hops mimicking your ready position
- Quick ladder-style footwork (even without a ladder, just rapid small steps in place)

**4. Left knee-specific considerations**
- Spend extra time on quad activation: wall sits for 15-20 seconds (not to fatigue, just to "turn on" the VMO)
- Light knee circles to lubricate the joint
- Consider a patella strap or light compression sleeve if you notice instability during lateral movement

**The key principle:** You want to be *neurally activated* but *metabolically fresh*. That means short bursts of intensity with full recovery between, not sustained effort that builds lactate. Think of it like turning the engine on and revving it briefly, not driving laps.

**What to avoid:**
- Long jogging warmups — these tire your legs without improving explosiveness
- Deep static stretches — save these for post-match
- Too many practice serves before the match starts (common energy drain)

If you're playing in cold weather, layer up during warmup and strip down right before play — cold muscles lose that "ready" feeling fast.\n
## human\n\nHow low should I be/ how much knee bend etc during match for ground strokes\n
## assistant\n\nThis is more about athletic positioning than going super low. Here's the practical breakdown:

**The right amount of bend**

Your knees should be bent enough that you could **spring in any direction instantly** — think "ready to pounce," not "sitting in a chair." Roughly a 20-30 degree knee bend in your ready position. If someone pushed you, you shouldn't fall — you should be able to absorb it.

**Ground strokes specifically:**

- **Loading phase** (split step + move to ball): Bend comes mostly from the hips and ankles, not just knees. Think "hinge at the hips" with knees tracking over toes. You're loading your legs like a spring.
- **Contact point**: You want your legs driving *up and through* the ball. The power in a groundstroke comes from the ground up — legs push into the court, energy transfers through core rotation into the arm. If your legs are straight at contact, you're arm-swinging.
- **Common mistake**: Going too low. This wastes energy, slows recovery for the next shot, and with your left knee, puts unnecessary shear force on the joint.

**A good mental cue:** Stay at a height where your **eyes are roughly at net height or slightly above**. That's usually the sweet spot — low enough for power generation, high enough for quick recovery.

**For your knee specifically:**

- Let the stronger (right) leg do more of the heavy loading on open-stance forehands
- On wide balls to your backhand side, try to avoid deep lunging onto the left leg — use more shuffle steps to arrive balanced rather than one big lunge
- Slight forward lean from the hips protects the knees more than deep knee bend does

**Energy management tip:** You don't need to be maximally loaded on every shot. Routine rally balls need maybe 60-70% leg engagement. Save the deep athletic loading for when you're hitting with intention — approach shots, passing shots, putaway balls. This keeps your legs fresh through the whole match.\n
