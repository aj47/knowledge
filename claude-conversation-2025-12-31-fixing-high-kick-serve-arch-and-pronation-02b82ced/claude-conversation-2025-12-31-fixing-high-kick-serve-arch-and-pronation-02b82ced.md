## Why Your Kick Serve Arcs Too High

A kick serve that balloons up usually comes from one or more of these:

1. **Brushing too much up, not enough out.** You're generating topspin but without enough forward momentum. Think of it as brushing up *and* through the ball rather than purely vertical.

2. **Contact point too far behind you.** If you're tossing too far over your head or making contact late, you'll naturally swing more upward. Try tossing slightly more into the court (still to the left of your head if you're right-handed, but not as far back).

3. **Not accelerating through contact.** A tentative swing produces a loopy, high arc. Commit to the swing—racket head speed is what keeps the ball driving forward with spin rather than floating.

4. **Insufficient knee drive.** If your legs aren't pushing you up and into the court, your arm has to do all the work vertically, which exaggerates the arc.

## Pronation on the Kick Serve

Pronation happens naturally if you set it up correctly—trying to force it often backfires. Here's how to let it happen:

- **Start with the edge.** As you swing up to the ball, your racket edge should be leading, almost like you're going to hit with the frame.
- **Brush up the back of the ball.** For a kick serve, you're brushing from roughly 7 o'clock to 1 o'clock (on a clock face, looking at the back of the ball).
- **Let the forearm rotate.** After contact, your palm will naturally rotate outward (away from your body). You'll finish with your palm facing away from you rather than toward the court.
- **Relax your grip.** A tight grip blocks pronation. Keep your hand loose enough that the racket can rotate through the hitting zone.

A good drill: practice the motion in slow motion without a ball, focusing on leading with the edge, brushing up, and letting the racket turn over naturally. Then gradually add speed.

Think about throwing a ball overhand. You don't consciously think about rotating your forearm, but it happens automatically as part of an efficient throwing motion. The serve works the same way. Your body naturally wants to pronate because it's the most efficient way to transfer energy through the kinetic chain.

Here's what's actually happening mechanically: as your arm accelerates upward and your elbow extends, your forearm is internally rotated (palm facing your head). At contact, the momentum of the swing combined with the relaxation in your wrist and forearm causes the forearm to rotate outward. The racket, being an extension of your arm, turns over as a result.

What blocks this natural rotation is tension. If you're gripping tightly or trying to muscle the ball, you lock up the wrist and forearm, and the racket can't turn freely. It's like trying to crack a whip while holding it rigidly—it doesn't work.

A helpful image: imagine your forearm is a door and your elbow is the hinge. As you swing up to contact, the door is closed (racket edge up). After contact, the door swings open naturally from the momentum. You're not pushing the door open—it opens because you're not holding it shut.

One thing that helps is focusing on the follow-through rather than the contact. If you think about finishing with your palm facing the right fence (for a righty), the pronation at contact tends to take care of itself. Trying to pronate *at* contact often leads to timing it wrong or forcing it.

## Frame 1: Approaching the Ball (just before contact)

Your racket is almost fully on edge. If you froze here, the strings would be facing toward your right ear, not the court. The angle of the racket face relative to the ground is nearly vertical—maybe 80 to 90 degrees. Someone standing behind you would see mostly the frame, not the strings.

Your arm is extending upward, elbow straightening, and the racket head is trailing slightly behind your hand due to the lag in your wrist. This lag is crucial—it stores elastic energy that's about to release.

## Frame 2: Contact

The racket has started to open but is still significantly closed compared to a flat serve. The strings now face roughly upward and slightly toward the left net post—maybe 45 to 60 degrees from vertical. You're not hitting through the back of the ball like a flat serve; you're brushing up and across it.

The contact point on the ball is lower than on a flat serve—think about catching the ball around its 7 or 8 o'clock position and exiting around 1 o'clock. Your wrist is still relatively laid back here, and pronation is just beginning.

Pronation is complete. The racket face is now nearly parallel to the ground or even past it, with strings facing right or even slightly behind you. Your arm continues across and down to your left side. The palm finishes facing completely away from your body.
